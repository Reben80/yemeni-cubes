What the piece has to be

Eight rotations of the cube permute the cells. The group acts freely: no rotation in it fixes a cell. That is what makes the eight pieces interchangeable. A piece has to meet these conditions.

  1. exactly 8 cells
  2. one cell from each of the 8 rotation orbits
  3. face-connected
  4. no sealed cavity — the complement stays connected to the outside
  5. no solid 2 × 2 × 2 block
  6. stricter reading: no 2 × 2 × 1 slab either. This one is optional. It is a filter, not a rule.

    34 of the 65 satisfy it. The other 31 are still Yemeni cubes. The catalogue can show only the thin ones.

  7. never touches itself only at an edge or corner (well-composedness)
  8. no tunnel through it (Euler characteristic = 1)

The eight orbits

Sixty-four cells, eight orbits, eight cells in each. A piece takes one cell from each orbit. The numbers are the orbits.

Where the name comes from

A Yemeni square is a square cut into four congruent polyominoes by a quarter turn about its centre. The name comes from a tiling pattern in Yemeni architecture. Wichmann and Rigby studied them. On a 4 × 4 square there are two: a T pinwheel and an L pinwheel. Both sit inside this cube, as a tetromino two layers thick.

In #1 the cube is two of those squares, stacked. Layers z = 0 and z = 1 match, and so do z = 2 and z = 3.
